Corn Snake Health Checklist: Feeding, Shedding, and Red Flags

July 7, 2026 7 min read By VetGPT

Corn snakes are the classic beginner snake for good reason: hardy, even-tempered, topping out at a manageable 3 to 5 feet, and easy to feed. But "beginner" does not mean bulletproof. Like every snake, a corn snake instinctively masks illness, and the two things new keepers most often get wrong are feeding size and telling a normal winter fast apart from a sick animal refusing food. Get your husbandry dialed in, keep a simple feeding and weight log, and a corn snake will thrive for 15 to 20 years.

Feeding by Age and Weight

The rule that prevents most feeding mistakes: the prey item should be roughly as wide as the widest part of your snake's body, leaving a small, gentle bulge after the meal. Too small and the snake stays hungry; too large and you risk regurgitation. Always feed frozen-thawed rodents rather than live. A live mouse can bite and seriously wound a snake, and thawed prey is safer, cheaper, and just as readily accepted.

🐭 Corn Snake Feeding Chart

  • Hatchling (under ~15 g): one pinky mouse every 5-7 days
  • Juvenile (15-40 g): fuzzy to hopper mouse every 7 days
  • Subadult (40-150 g): hopper to weaned/adult mouse every 7-10 days
  • Adult (150 g and up): one adult mouse every 10-14 days
  • Prey width = the widest part of the snake; expect a slight bulge
  • Always frozen-thawed, warmed to body temperature, never live
  • Do not handle for 48 hours after a meal to prevent regurgitation

Weigh your snake monthly on a kitchen scale and log it. Weight is the single number that tells you whether your feeding schedule is right and whether a fast is harmless or a warning.

Brumation Versus Illness Refusal

Corn snakes commonly stop eating in the cooler months. As temperatures and daylight drop, many enter a brumation-like slowdown, hunkering down and refusing food for weeks. This is normal and, in a healthy adult, not dangerous. The problem is that food refusal is also one of the first signs of illness, so keepers panic when they should not, or relax when they should not. The deciding factor is weight and demeanor, not appetite alone.

❄️ Normal Fast vs. Concerning Refusal

  • Normal: weight holds steady week to week, snake is simply slow and hides more
  • Normal: clear eyes, clean vent, normal posture, no breathing sounds
  • Concerning: steady weight loss over several weeks
  • Concerning: lethargy that is not just cool-season slowdown
  • Concerning: wheezing, gaping, mucus, or star-gazing posture
  • When in doubt, check that warm-side temps are 85-88°F; cold can suppress appetite

A corn snake that refuses food but holds its weight and looks bright is almost always fine. One that is losing weight, sitting oddly, or showing any respiratory sign is not brumating and needs a vet. This is exactly why the monthly weight log matters so much.

Shed Tracking and Skin Health

A corn snake's shed cycle is a reliable window into its health and hydration. The cycle runs roughly: skin dulls, the belly takes on a pinkish cast, the eyes cloud over blue or milky, then the eyes clear, and the snake sheds within about 4 to 7 days. A healthy shed comes off in one complete piece, inside out, from nose to tail, including both eye caps. Track when the blue phase starts, when the eyes clear, and whether the shed was complete.

Incomplete or patchy sheds point to low humidity, dehydration, or an underlying problem. Provide a humid hide (a box with damp sphagnum moss) during shed cycles and keep ambient humidity around 40 to 50 percent, rising during a shed. After every shed, confirm both eye caps came off and check the tail tip, where retained shed likes to build up in tight bands and can cut off circulation over time.

See a reptile vet for: Wheezing, gaping, or open-mouth breathing (respiratory infection) · Mucus or bubbles at the mouth or nostrils · Progressive weight loss · Repeated regurgitation · Brown, red, or blistered belly scales (scale rot) · Retained eye caps or stuck shed constricting the tail · Tiny moving mites along the scale seams · Star-gazing or an inability to right itself · Any lump, swelling, or open wound

Scale Rot and Enclosure Hygiene

Scale rot is a bacterial infection of the belly scales, and it is almost always a husbandry problem. It starts when substrate stays too wet or dirty and the snake sits in constant moisture and waste. The first sign is discoloration of the ventral scales, brown or red patches that can progress to blisters and open sores. Caught early, fixing the enclosure and drying it out may be enough; advanced cases need a reptile vet and antibiotics, because the infection can go systemic.

Prevent it with simple hygiene: spot-clean waste daily, use an appropriate dry substrate like aspen, do a full substrate change regularly, and make sure the warm side is not creating a swampy microclimate. Track your cleaning and shed events alongside feeding and weight so a pattern jumps out before it becomes a health crisis. Common Questions

How often should I feed my corn snake?

Hatchlings eat a pinky every 5 to 7 days, juveniles a fuzzy or hopper every 7 days, and adults one appropriately sized adult mouse every 10 to 14 days. The prey should be about the width of the widest part of the snake. Feed frozen-thawed, never live, to avoid bite injuries.

Why is my corn snake refusing to eat?

Common harmless reasons include an upcoming shed, cooler winter temperatures triggering a brumation-like slowdown, a new enclosure, or being handled too soon after a meal. Refusal becomes a concern when paired with weight loss, lethargy, or breathing sounds. Monthly weigh-ins tell the two apart.

How can I tell brumation from a sick snake?

A brumating corn snake refuses food but holds a stable weight, is just slow and hidden, and has clear eyes and a clean vent. A snake losing weight, wheezing, gaping, or sitting in a strange posture is not brumating. Stable weight over weeks is the key sign that a fast is normal.

What is scale rot in corn snakes?

Scale rot is a bacterial skin infection of the belly scales caused by substrate that is too wet or dirty. It shows as brown, red, or blistered belly scales. Dry out and clean the enclosure and see a reptile vet, because advanced scale rot needs antibiotics and can become systemic.
