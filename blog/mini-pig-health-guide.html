Mini Pig Health: Diet, Weight, and Hoof Care

July 7, 2026 7 min read By VetGPT

The most important thing to understand about mini pigs is a marketing lie: there is no such thing as a teacup, micro, or pocket pig. The palm-sized piglet in the photo is a baby, and a healthy adult potbellied pig lands somewhere between 70 and 150 pounds and keeps growing until it is 4 or 5 years old. The truly tiny adults you occasionally see got that way through deliberate underfeeding, which wrecks their bones, joints, and organs. If you keep a mini pig, you are keeping a smart, food-motivated, long-lived animal (12 to 20 years) that will outweigh most dogs. Nearly every health problem these pigs develop traces back to two things owners get wrong: how much they feed, and neglecting the feet.

Pigs are also intensely intelligent and will train you faster than you train them. A pig that has learned screaming produces snacks becomes an overweight, pushy pig. Structure, portions, and routine care are the whole game.

Diet and Weight: The Core Problem

Obesity is the number one health issue in pet pigs, and it is entirely owner-created. Overfed pigs pack fat around the eyes until fat rolls literally blind them, strain their joints, and develop heart and liver disease. The opposite extreme, the starved teacup, is just as damaging. You want a lean, level-backed pig where you can feel the ribs and hip bones under a light fat cover, not see them and not bury them.

🐷 Feeding and Weight Checklist

  • Feed a commercial mini pig pellet made for potbellied pigs, not dog food or hog grower
  • Portion by weight and body condition: roughly 1-1.5 cups per day for an average adult, split into two meals
  • Never free-feed and never leave food down; measure every meal
  • Add leafy greens and limited vegetables for volume; keep fruit and treats minimal
  • Body condition target: ribs and hips felt with light pressure, a level back, no fat pads over the eyes
  • Weigh or photograph body condition monthly to catch creep early
  • Provide a shallow water source and rooting opportunities to keep the pig busy

Because pigs are so food-driven, cutting weight is hard once they are heavy. The fix is prevention: measure meals from day one, refuse to reward begging, and track body condition monthly. A pig whose eyes are being crowded by fat pads is already well down the wrong road.

Hoof and Tusk Care

Wild and pasture pigs wear their hooves down on rough, varied ground. A pet pig on grass, carpet, and soft yard does not, so the hooves overgrow, curl, and change how the pig stands, which strains joints and can cause lasting lameness. Plan on a hoof trim every 6 to 12 months, done by a vet or an experienced pig trimmer, often with light sedation because a pig that does not want its feet touched is strong and loud. Intact and even neutered males grow tusks that keep erupting; these may need trimming every 1 to 2 years to prevent them cutting the pig's own face or catching on things. Do not try to yank or fully cut a tusk at the gumline, which exposes the pulp; a vet trims them safely.

Dippity Pig and Other Quirks

One alarming but usually benign condition is dippity pig syndrome, medically erythema multiforme. A pig, often a younger one, suddenly develops a painful back, dips or buckles in the hindquarters, may scream and refuse to walk, and shows red, weepy streaks along the spine. It typically follows stress or sun exposure and usually resolves on its own within 24 to 48 hours. It looks like a spinal emergency but rarely is; still, a first episode should be checked by a vet to rule out a real back injury. Keep the pig calm, cool, and quiet while it passes. Mini pigs are also prone to dry, flaky skin and need shade and sometimes pig-safe sunscreen, since their sparse hair offers little sun protection.

See a vet for: A pig off feed (a pig refusing food is almost always seriously ill) · Repeated vomiting or straining to pass stool or urine · Sudden hind-end weakness or dipping that does not resolve in a day · Labored breathing or blue-tinged skin · Overgrown, curling hooves or a tusk cutting the face · Rapid weight gain with fat crowding the eyes · A hard, distended belly · Any seizure, collapse, or fever

Legality, Vets, and Records

Before anything else, check your zoning. Many cities and counties classify pigs as livestock and ban them on residential lots regardless of size, and people learn this only after bonding with the animal and then face surrender or a forced move. Confirm in writing that a pig is allowed where you live, and understand that a mini pig is a decade-plus commitment to a large, intelligent animal, not a novelty. Just as important, line up a veterinarian who actually treats pigs before you need one. Pig-savvy vets are not on every corner, and finding one mid-emergency is a bad time to start looking. Common Questions

How big does a mini pig actually get?

There is no teacup, micro, or pocket pig. A healthy adult mini pig, or potbelly, usually weighs 70 to 150 pounds and grows until about 4 to 5 years old. The tiny piglets sold as teacups are underfed or bred from starved parents and grow into full-size pigs. Plan for a 100-pound-plus animal living 12 to 20 years.

What should I feed a mini pig?

Feed a commercial mini pig pellet made for potbellied pigs, measured by weight and body condition, usually around 1 to 1.5 cups a day for an average adult split into two meals, plus leafy greens and limited vegetables. Never free-feed and never feed dog food or lots of fruit. Obesity is the most common health problem in pet pigs.

Do mini pigs need their hooves trimmed?

Yes. Pet pigs do not wear hooves down naturally, so they overgrow and need trimming roughly every 6 to 12 months to keep the pig walking correctly. Males also grow tusks that may need trimming every 1 to 2 years. Most owners have a vet or experienced trimmer do this, sometimes under mild sedation.

What is dippity pig syndrome?

Dippity pig syndrome, or erythema multiforme, is a sudden condition where a pig's back becomes painful, it dips or wobbles in the hindquarters, may scream, and sometimes cannot walk, often with red weepy streaks along the spine. It usually follows stress or sun in younger pigs and resolves on its own in a day or two, but a vet should confirm it is not a spinal injury.
